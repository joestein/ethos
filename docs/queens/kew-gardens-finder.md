## 1.1 THE BOUNDARY CONFLICT — this is the single most important item in the file

Two sources of very different weight give **materially different** boundaries for Kew Gardens.
They are not reconcilable by wording alone: they disagree about the eastern and southern edges.

**F1.** `[Q-PDF]` **NYC Landmarks Preservation Commission**, *Ralph Bunche House Designation
Report*, LP-2175, May 17, 2005, Designation List 363, p. 3, section headed "Kew Gardens":
> "Located in central Queens, the area known as Kew Gardens is bounded by the Jackie Robinson
> (originally Interborough) Parkway and Queens Boulevard on the north, Kew Gardens Road on the east,
> Myrtle Avenue on the south and Forest Park on the west."
Source: http://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/2175.pdf
The report footnotes this section (note 11) to: Barry Lewis, *Kew Gardens: Urban Village in the Big

# 2. HISTORY — founding, the Man/Mann family, the garden-suburb plan

The richest single vein in this file is the LPC designation report LP-2175, which contains a
dedicated "Kew Gardens" neighborhood-history section. It is institutional, footnoted, and I read it
directly off the PDF, so its quotes are the most trustworthy here.

**F21.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 3:
> "The development of this area began when a new rail line for the Long Island Railroad was
> established in the 1860s, linking the outer reaches of Long Island to a ferry in Queens that would
> transport passengers into Manhattan. This expansion made large tracts of land in Queens attractive
> to developers, one of whom was New York lawyer Albon P. Mann."

**F22.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 3:
> "In 1869 Mann purchased five large farmsteads north of the Jamaica Turnpike. Over the course of
> the next forty years, Mann, along with landscape architect Edward Richmond, created the
> development known as Richmond Hill on the southern part of this property. The area north of 84th
> Avenue was initially set aside as the Richmond Hill Country Club (demolished)."
**CONFLICT (date + spelling):** LPC says **1869** and spells the name **Mann**. Wikipedia's Kew
Gardens and Richmond Hill articles both say **1868** and spell it **Man** (Albon P. Man / Albon
Platt Man). Forgotten New York also uses **Man**. Record both; do not silently harmonize.

**F23.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 3:
> "By 1910, a new rail line was run through this northern section and a new rail station was opened
> in an area called Kew Gardens, named after the Royal Botanical Gardens at Kew, in England."

**F24.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 3 — the fullest account of the garden-suburb plan anywhere in
this file:
> "Kew Gardens was developed by Albon Mann's three sons, Alrick, Arthur and Albon, Jr. during the
> next thirty years. The Mann brothers invested significantly in amenities for the community, hoping
> to attract enough wealthy buyers to bring a significant return on their investment. They helped
> establish important institutions such as the Kew Gardens Country Club, Forest Park, the community
> church and the local elementary school. They built a commercial area, so they could set the
> architectural style of these buildings, and also so they could collect the rent for themselves. It
> consisted of a neo-Tudor style village center with stores on the ground story and apartments
> above, all centered on the rail station. The Manns laid out the original streets in a winding
> system that reflected the area's topography, and planted pin oaks along the streets for added
> greenery. Since they could not do all the development at once themselves, they sold off parcels to
> other developers, but always with restrictive covenants to control what was constructed. As a
> result, the neighborhood filled with a variety of housing types: apartments, townhouses, and
> single-family buildings ranging in size from cottages to mansions. In this way, the Mann brothers
> attracted upper and lower middle class residents who wanted the benefits of a garden suburb. The
> facilities and institutions they developed helped create a sense of community among the residents,
> who often were people of artistic pursuits, as well as those of a variety of religions and
> ethnicities."
**Note for the writer:** this passage is the source for "garden suburb," "neo-Tudor village center,"
"restrictive covenants," "pin oaks," and "winding streets." It also names a **Kew Gardens Country
Club** — distinct from the **Richmond Hill Country Club** in F22. Both are described as
Mann-family institutions. Neither appears to survive; do not imply either is extant.

**F25.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 1 (Summary):
> "Kew Gardens was a garden city type development begun in the early years of the twentieth century
> that tended to attract open-minded residents and was racially integrated. The area had a mix of
> types and styles of homes, mostly historical revival styles, set in a lush, suburban atmosphere."

**F26.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, note 12 (p. 6), on why the rail line moved:
> "The Pennsylvania Railroad took over the Long Island Railroad in 1901 and proceeded to electrify
> the lines. This required a realignment and thus new tracks between Long Island City and Jamaica."

**F27.** `[Q]` Wikipedia, Kew Gardens, Queens, on the naming decision by Man's heirs:
> "decided to lay out a new community and called it at first Kew and then Kew Gardens after the
> well-known botanical gardens in England."
`[P]` Wikipedia names the heirs "Aldrick Man and Albon Man Jr." — note this differs from LPC's
"Alrick, Arthur and Albon, Jr." (three sons vs. two; "Aldrick" vs. "Alrick"). Flag.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kew_Gardens,_Queens

**F28.** `[Q]` Wikipedia: "Kew Gardens was one of seven planned garden communities built in Queens
from the late 19th century to 1950." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kew_Gardens,_Queens

**F29.** `[P]` Wikipedia timeline of early development: Albon P. Man acquires much of the area 1868;
Maple Grove Cemetery opens 1875 and an LIRR station called **Hopedale** is built to serve mourners;

## P1 — Ralph Johnson Bunche House (National Historic Landmark + NYC individual landmark)

**Address:** 115-24 Grosvenor Road, Kew Gardens, Queens, NY 11415.
**Neighborhood per sources:** Kew Gardens — unanimous across LPC, NPS/NRHP and Wikipedia.
**NOT contested.** This is the marquee landmark of the neighborhood.

**F45.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175 title line: "RALPH BUNCHE HOUSE, 115-24 Grosvenor Road, Kew Gardens,
Queens. Built, 1927; Architects, Koch & Wagner." Landmark Site: "Borough of Queens Tax Map Block
3319, Lot 18." Designated May 17, 2005, Designation List 363.
Source: http://s-media.nyc.gov/agencies/lpc/lp/2175.pdf

**F46.** `[P]` NRHP reference #76001266, listed May 11, 1976; designated a National Historic Landmark
the same date; New York State Register June 23, 1980; NYC individual landmark May 17, 2005.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Johnson_Bunche_House ;
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places_listings_in_Queens_County,_New_York
**⚑ Address discrepancy:** the NRHP listings table renders the address as **"115-125 Grosvenor Rd."**
while LPC, the NYC landmarks list and Wikipedia's article all say **115-24 Grosvenor Road**. The
"115-125" form is almost certainly a transcription artifact of the hyphenated Queens address. Flag,
do not silently correct.

**F47.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175 Summary, p. 1:
> "Dr. Ralph Bunche and his family lived for more than thirty years in a neo-Tudor style residence
> constructed in 1927 and designed by the prominent Brooklyn architects Koch & Wagner, located in
> Kew Gardens, Queens."

**F48.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 3, on how the Bunches got here:
> "Upon the establishment of the United Nations, Bunche and his family moved from Washington, D.C.
> to New York in 1947. They first lived in Jamaica, Queens, before buying this house in Kew Gardens
> in 1952."
**Important for this wave:** the Bunche family's FIRST New York home was **Parkway Village in
Jamaica**, 1947–1952 — that belongs to the Jamaica file, not this one. LPC note 5 describes Parkway
Village: `[Q-PDF]` "Parkway Village, designed by Leonard Schultze, consists of 107 two and three
story apartment buildings on approximately 40 acres of land. The development was built with the
support of the United Nations so that there would be housing for its many staff members who would
otherwise not be able to rent in Manhattan's segregated housing market."

**F49.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 3:
> "In 1952, Ralph and Ruth Bunche purchased a neo-Tudor style house nearby in Kew Gardens, Queens.
> Mrs. Bunche had found the house while Ralph was incapacitated by phlebitis. ... The owner of the
> house was an admirer of Mr. Bunche and lowered the price for him, as well as offering to sell him
> anything in the house that they wanted. The family was quite happy with the house and gardens and
> lived in this house until Mrs. Bunche's death in 1988."

**F50.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 3, on why he chose an integrated neighborhood:
> "Although Bunche had been offered a job as Under Secretary of State in the Truman administration,
> he did not want to return to the segregation of the District of Columbia. In New York, Bunche was
> able to live in an integrated neighborhood, first with other UN families and later in a
> comfortable, early twentieth century railroad suburb."

**F51.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 4, on the architects:
> "The Brooklyn architectural firm of Koch & Wagner was chosen to design the house. Arthur R. Koch
> (1874-1952) and Charles C. Wagner (1876-1957) had formed a partnership in 1910 which they
> maintained until 1951, when Wagner retired to Florida."
and
> "While they did create some residential designs, a free-standing suburban house was a rather
> unusual type for this firm."

**F52.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175 Description, p. 5:
> "The house at 115-24 Grovesnor Road is a 2 ½ story neo-Tudor style residence faced in stucco
> pierced by random bricks and stones. Some wooden half-timbering is located near the tops of the
> gables. The house maintains its original wooden doors with iron strapping and original leaded
> glass windows and slate roof."
(sic — the report itself misspells "Grovesnor" in this sentence.)
`[P]` Further description: set on the crest of a hill with a terraced flagstone walkway; attached

**F53.** `[Q-PDF]` LPC LP-2175, p. 1, quoting U Thant on Bunche: "An international institution in his
own right, transcending both nationality and race in a way that is achieved by very few."

**F54.** `[P]` Bunche facts from the LPC report worth checking before use: born Detroit 1903, orphaned
1917; UCLA on full scholarship, summa cum laude and class valedictorian; 1934 the first African

## P2 — Kew Gardens LIRR station and the Lefferts Boulevard bridge shops

**Address:** near Austin Street and Lefferts Boulevard, Kew Gardens.
**Neighborhood:** Kew Gardens — consistent across Wikipedia, MTA-derived sources, FNY and qns.com.
This is the strongest "travel for it" candidate after the Bunche House.

**F56.** `[Q]` Wikipedia: the station has "two high-level side platforms, each six cars long"; four

# 6. EXPLICIT NEGATIVES AND OFF-LIMITS ITEMS

* **No waterfront** (F15). Kew Gardens is inland; Jamaica Bay is not its water.
* **No NYC or NRHP historic district** (F16, F18).
* **No public library branch** (F142) — the nearest are Richmond Hill and Briarwood.
* **No fire station** (F144).
* **The police station house is in Richmond Hill** (F145), though the precinct serves Kew Gardens.
* **No museum.** I found no museum or cultural institution with a building in Kew Gardens. The
  cultural life documented here is the Kew Gardens Cinemas (P17), the Friends of Maple Grove
  Cemetery's programming (F82), and the annual Community Arts Day (P18). That is the honest picture.
* **Flushing Meadows–Corona Park is settled and off-limits**, as are the eight
  Ethos.Seeds.CitiFieldPlaces holdings — as records AND as prose. Wikipedia's Kew Gardens article
  lists Flushing Meadows–Corona Park as lying "to the north" (F4) and the Kew Gardens Interchange
  article names it as a bordering area (F106). **Neither is a licence to narrate anything inside it.**
  A routing link to /g/citi-field-guide is the only correct move if one is warranted at all.

# 8. RICHEST VEINS OF MATERIAL (finder's assessment of where the substance is, not a verdict)

1. **LPC designation report LP-2175** — an institutional, footnoted, three-paragraph history of the
   Kew Gardens garden-suburb plan (F21–F25, F36), plus the full Bunche story. Nothing else in this
   file comes close for reliability. It cites Barry Lewis's *Kew Gardens: Urban Village in the Big
   City* (Kew Gardens Council for the Recreation and the Arts, 1999) as its underlying source.
2. **The Lefferts Boulevard bridge shops** — a genuinely singular structure (three bridges, shops
   hung from them "like a curtain on a rod"), with a documented 2018–2021 fight over demolition,
   named legislators, dollar figures and direct quotes. F58–F63.
3. **The Ralph Bunche House** — NHL + NRHP + NYC individual landmark, with an architect, a date, a
   detailed description, and a moving account of why an integrated Queens suburb mattered to him.
   F45–F55.
4. **Maple Grove Cemetery** — NYC Parks' historical sign is unusually rich (founding, Prospect
   Cemetery overcrowding, the Long Island Democrat of Feb 9 1875, the first interment, five named
   burials with biographies). F75–F83. Marred only by the three-way neighborhood conflict.
5. **The Genovese case, told as myth-and-correction** — the *Times*' own 2016 retraction and the 2007
   scholarship give a page a defensible, non-lurid way to handle the neighborhood's most famous
   association. F113–F119.
6. **Civic Virtue → Women's Plaza** — a complete arc: 1922 City Hall, La Guardia's "Fat Boy," 1941
   exile to Queens, 2012 removal to Green-Wood, and a plaza named for women on the same spot.
   F98–F101.
7. **The street-name layer** — the vanished alphabetical English names (Ibis, Juno, Kingsley,
   Lefferts, Mowbray, Newbold, Onslow, Pembroke, Quentin) and the survivors, with Mowbray's
   off-grid house numbers. F37–F39.
