# London Scoping — Freedom of Panorama

**Wave:** London scoping, angle 1
**Date:** 2026-09-04
**Question:** the spec (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-04-london-boroughs-design.md`,
§ "What differs for London" #2) records a *belief* that s.62 CDPA 1988 is broader than both
Italy's absence of FoP and 17 USC 120(a), and refuses to treat it as settled. This file settles it.

**Verdict up front: the broader reading HOLDS, and it is broader than the spec guessed in one
respect and narrower in another.** Broader — "building" is statutorily defined to include *any
fixed structure*, and the exception reaches indoor works in premises merely *open to the public*,
so museum and lobby sculpture is in. Narrower — the exception is gated on **permanent** situation,
which knocks out an entire class the spec did not anticipate: rotating public-art commissions,
of which London's most famous is the Fourth Plinth.

---

## 1. The operative text

Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s.62. Extent: **U.K.** Fetched from
<https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/62> (latest revised version; the page states
"There are currently no known outstanding effects for the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988,
Section 62"). Quoted verbatim:

> **62 Representation of certain artistic works on public display.**
>
> (1) This section applies to—
>   (a) buildings, and
>   (b) sculptures, models for buildings and works of artistic craftsmanship,
>   if permanently situated in a public place or in premises open to the public.
>
> (2) The copyright in such a work is not infringed by—
>   (a) making a graphic work representing it,
>   (b) making a photograph or film of it, or
>   (c) [F1 making a broadcast of] a visual image of it.
>
> (3) Nor is the copyright infringed by the issue to the public of copies, or the
> [F2 communication to the public], of anything whose making was, by virtue of this section, not
> an infringement of the copyright.

Amendments F1 and F2 are the 2003 InfoSoc-implementing substitutions (S.I. 2003/2498), which
replaced broadcast/cable wording — they do not narrow the categories.

**Subsection (3) is the load-bearing sentence for this corpus.** It is not a private-study or
non-commercial carve-out. It says in terms that *issuing copies to the public* and *communicating
to the public* the resulting photograph is likewise not an infringement. There is no attribution
condition, no commercial-use exclusion, and no compensation scheme. VERIFIED from the statute.

### The definitions that do the work — s.4 CDPA

Fetched from <https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/4>, verbatim:

> **4 Artistic works.**
> (1) In this Part "artistic work" means—
>   (a) a graphic work, photograph, sculpture or collage, irrespective of artistic quality,
>   (b) a work of architecture being a building or a model for a building, or
>   (c) a work of artistic craftsmanship.
>
> (2) In this Part—
> "**building**" includes any fixed structure, and a part of a building or fixed structure;
> "**graphic work**" includes— (a) any painting, drawing, diagram, map, chart or plan, and
> (b) any engraving, etching, lithograph, woodcut or similar work;
> "**photograph**" means a recording of light or other radiation on any medium…
> "**sculpture**" includes a cast or model made for purposes of sculpture.

Two consequences, both VERIFIED from the statute rather than inferred:

- **"Building" is enormous.** *Any fixed structure* and *any part of* one. A bridge, a gateway, a
  bandstand, a pier, a fountain basin, a bollard, a station canopy, a lamp standard — all fixed
  structures. This is why the UK exception is more permissive than 17 USC 120(a), which is limited
  to "architectural works" as defined in 17 USC 101.
- **s.4(1)(a) lists "graphic work" as an artistic work, and s.62(1) conspicuously does not
  list it.** The omission is deliberate and is the whole of the murals-and-street-art problem.

### What "permanently situated in a public place or in premises open to the public" means

No UK case defines it and I could not find one. The best available authority is the standard
practitioner text, quoted by Wikimedia Commons at
<https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Copyright_rules_by_territory/United_Kingdom#Freedom_of_panorama>:

> According to *Copinger and Skone James*, the expression "open to the public" presumably extends
> the section to premises to which the public are admitted only on licence or on payment.
> — *Copinger and Skone James on Copyright* (18th edn, Sweet & Maxwell 2021) vol 1, para 9-289

So, in practice:

- **"public place OR premises open to the public"** is a disjunction. Ticketed and licensed
  premises count. Commons draws the operational conclusion explicitly: "it is acceptable to upload
  to Commons not only photographs of public buildings and sculptures but also works of artistic
  craftsmanship which are on permanent public display in museums, galleries and exhibitions which
  are open to the public."
- **"Permanently situated"** is the real gate, and it is about the *work's* tenure at the site,
  not about the photographer's access. Commons practice (§4 below) treats a fixed-term public-art
  commission as failing it, and treats a work that has *moved* to permanent display as passing it
  at the new site while still failing at the old one.

## 2. What s.62 does NOT cover

Answering the spec's four probes plus the ones that matter more.

**A mural — is it a graphic work, and therefore outside s.62? YES.** A mural is a painting;
"graphic work" *includes* "any painting" (s.4(2)). s.62(1) names buildings, sculptures, models for
buildings and works of artistic craftsmanship, and does not name graphic works. Commons states it
flatly:

> The freedom provided by Section 62 does not apply to graphic works — such as a mural or poster —
> even if they are permanently located in a public place. These cannot be uploaded to Commons
> without a licence from the copyright holder.

The Commons summary box for the UK reads: "✔ for 3D works · ✔ for 2D 'works of artistic
craftsmanship' · ✘ for 2D 'graphic works'". VERIFIED.

**A sculpture in a building's lobby — is it permanently situated in a public place? Wrong
question, but it is covered anyway.** A lobby is unlikely to be a "public place"; it does not need
to be. s.62's second limb is "or in premises open to the public", and per Copinger that reaches
premises entered on licence or on payment. If the lobby is one the public may enter and the
sculpture lives there, s.62 applies. INFERRED from the statutory disjunction plus Copinger; no
case authority found. The failure mode is not "indoors" but "temporary" — a loaned or rotating
lobby piece fails.

**A war memorial — covered.** It is a sculpture, or on the wider limb a fixed structure, and it is
permanently situated by definition. Note that most London war memorials are additionally out of
copyright on duration alone (life + 70): the Cenotaph is Lutyens, d. 1944, so PD from 1 Jan 2015.
s.62 is the belt; expiry is the braces.

**A fountain — covered twice over.** It is a sculpture and it is a fixed structure. See the Eros
worked example below.

**A blue plaque — I could not establish this and the honest answer is "do not rely on s.62".**
The reasoning, marked INFERRED throughout: a ceramic roundel bearing lettering is most naturally a
*graphic work* (lettering and a border — cf. "drawing"), which would put it outside s.62; it is
not obviously a sculpture, and calling it a "work of artistic craftsmanship" runs into *Hensher v
Restawile* [1976] AC 64, which requires the maker to be "both a craftsman and an artist". The
practical rescue is that a standard roundel with plain text almost certainly fails the threshold
of originality — after *THJ v Sheridan* [2023] EWCA Civ 1354 the UK test is "author's own
intellectual creation", which Commons notes is "more demanding" than the old skill-and-labour
test. A separate, non-copyright issue is that the English Heritage blue plaque is a branded
device; **I could not verify whether it is a registered trade mark** and did not assert it. Net:
a plaque photographed *as part of* a building elevation is fine on the building limb; a photograph
*of the plaque itself* is a judgement call the gate should not have to make. Rule below excludes it.

**Other things s.62 does not do.** It does not extinguish the *photographer's* copyright — a
licence for the photograph is still required (Commons: "the exception does not eliminate the need
for a license from the photographer"). It does not touch moral rights (ss.77–80 CDPA), trade
marks, personality rights, or land-owner byelaws. It does not reach works in private places not
open to the public. And it does not reach temporary installations of any kind — the Serpentine
Pavilion, built and demolished annually, is on my reading in the same position as the Fourth
Plinth (INFERRED; not separately tested at Commons in what I found).

## 3. Street art, and Banksy specifically

**Conclusion: a photograph of a Banksy on a public wall may NOT be published commercially in
reliance on s.62, and a photograph that features one should not enter this corpus.**

What it turns on, in order:

1. **Categorisation, and this is the whole game.** Banksy's street work is stencilled
   spray-painting. That is a painting, hence a "graphic work" (s.4(2)), hence outside s.62(1),
   which names only buildings, sculptures, models for buildings and works of artistic
   craftsmanship. The permanence and the publicness of the wall are irrelevant once
   categorisation fails. VERIFIED from statute; Commons agrees, tagging its UK street-art
   deletions "There is no freedom of panorama in the UK for 2D graphic works such as this".

2. **Subsistence and ownership.** Commons' subject-matter guidance
   (<https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Copyright_rules_by_subject_matter#Graffiti>) cites
   *Creative Foundation v Dreamland Leisure Ltd* (2015), in which the High Court "indicated
   *obiter* that copyright in the mural belongs *prima facie* to Banksy". Banksy is living, so
   life + 70 is not remotely in play.

3. **Illegality is not a defence you can rely on.** The argument that copyright in unlawfully
   applied graffiti is unenforceable is untested in England and Wales. Commons notes that some
   jurisdictions legislate the point — "All illegal graffiti in Brazil are not protected by
   copyright" — which is precisely the kind of provision the UK does *not* have.

4. **Incidental inclusion (s.31) rescues almost nothing here.** s.31(1): "Copyright in a work is
   not infringed by its incidental inclusion in an artistic work, sound recording, film [or
   broadcast]" (<https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/31>). It applies only where
   inclusion is genuinely incidental. Commons rejected the de minimis argument in the Brexit-mural
   deletion in terms worth quoting because it is exactly the test an image agent should apply:
   *"There is nothing incidental about the inclusion of the painting of the man. Showcasing it is
   the whole point of these images."* A Shoreditch streetscape in which a Banksy is one small
   element among many may survive; a photograph whose subject is the Banksy does not.

5. **The EUIPO litigation is about TRADE MARKS and must not be cited as a copyright ruling.**
   Banksy's company Pest Control Office lost EU trade mark proceedings brought by the greetings-card
   firm Full Colour Black. Per BBC News, 17 September 2020
   (<https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/entertainment-arts-54189113>), the EU trade mark office threw out
   the "flower bomber" mark, "saying he had filed it in order to avoid using copyright laws, which
   are separate and would have required [him] to reveal his true identity. The ruling quoted from
   one of his books, in which he said 'copyright is for losers'." **That is a bad-faith finding
   against a trade mark registration.** It does not adjudicate copyright, does not place the mural
   in the public domain, and does not license anyone to photograph it. The Commons nominator made
   the same point pre-emptively in 2019, linking a piece headed "Banksy finally goes to court to
   stop unauthorised merchandising despite saying copyright is for losers".

**Commons has acted on all of this, recently and at scale.** The mass nomination
`Commons:Deletion requests/Files in Category:Banksy in London` (opened 17 November 2025) was
closed **31 December 2025**: *"Deleted: most per nomination — Kept the two that were struck, plus
File:What are you looking at.jpg, which is just plain text."* The two survivors were kept precisely
because, on inspection, "it doesn't appear there are any 2D artworks in these two images". The
earlier `Files in Category:Brexit mural (Banksy)` DR was closed **Deleted, 24 April 2019**.

**One caution for the finder wave.** Not every file on Commons tagged "Banksy" has been cleaned
up. `File:Image of Banksy street art in Walthamstow, London.jpg` is presently on Commons under a
bare `{{self|cc-zero}}` self-licence by the photographer, with no FoP tag and no acknowledgement
of the underlying artwork. It is a survivor of an incomplete sweep, not a licence. **A CC0 tag on
a Commons file is evidence about the photograph, never about the artwork in it.**

## 4. What Wikimedia Commons does about the UK

Country page: <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Copyright_rules_by_territory/United_Kingdom>
(shortcut COM:CRT/United Kingdom; the FoP anchor is COM:FOP UK).

**Infobox summary field, verbatim:** `Freedom of panorama = For 3D works and "works of artistic
craftsmanship"`. Standard term "Life + 70 years".

**The FoP section's own summary, verbatim:**

> ✔ for 3D works
> ✔ for 2D "works of artistic craftsmanship"
> ✘ for 2D "graphic works" {{FoP-UK}}
>
> Section 62 of the UK *Copyright, Designs and Patents Act* 1988 is broader than the corresponding
> provisions in many other countries, and allows photographers to take pictures of buildings, and
> sculptures, models for buildings and works of artistic craftsmanship (if permanently situated in
> a public place or in premises open to the public) without breaching copyright. **Such photographs
> may be published in any way.**

It also records the point the spec's guess missed: *"The word 'building' is quite broadly defined
by section 4(2), and includes 'any fixed structure, and a part of a building or fixed structure'."*

### The tag

**`{{FoP-UK}}`** — <https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Template:FoP-UK>. English text, verbatim
from `Template:FoP-UK/en`:

> The photographic reproduction of this work is covered under United Kingdom law (Section 62 of
> the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988), which states that it is not an infringement to
> take photographs of buildings, or of sculptures, models for buildings, or works of artistic
> craftsmanship permanently located in a public place or in premises open to the public. This does
> not apply to two-dimensional graphic works such as posters or murals.

It categorises into `Category:FoP-United Kingdom` and is a **restriction** tag: it sits alongside
the photograph's own licence, it does not replace it. The idiomatic construction on a well-formed
file is `{{Licensed-FOP|{{FoP-UK}}|{{self|cc-by-sa-4.0}}}}` — which is exactly what
`File:The Shard from the Sky Garden 2015.jpg` carries. **A file relying on UK FoP should show both
halves; a file showing only a photographer's CC licence has not been assessed for the underlying
work.**

The four copyright tags the country page lists in its infobox — `{{PD-old-auto}}`, `{{PD-UKGov}}`,
`{{PD-UK-unknown}}`, `{{PD-UK-posthumous-non-photo-1996}}` — are duration/PD tags, not FoP tags,
and they are the alternative route for pre-1955-ish sculptors.

**The instructive counter-example.** `Commons:Deletion requests/Files in Category:Nelson's Ship in
a Bottle` (Yinka Shonibare, Fourth Plinth 2010–2012) was nominated on the same temporariness
ground. The images shot **at the National Maritime Museum in Greenwich, where the work is now
permanently sited**, are struck through in the nomination list and were kept; the images shot on
the plinth in Trafalgar Square were not. Keep rationale: *"The 4th plinth location might have been
a temporary one, but it is still on display at the National Maritime Museum … I think it counts as
being on permanent public display, it's just that it's been placed in several different
locations."* **The test is where the work permanently is, not where it once stood.**

### Non-copyright restrictions — is there a UK analogue of the Italian problem?

The Rome wave found that Commons treats non-copyright restrictions as the reuser's problem, and
the general policy confirms it (<https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Non-copyright_restrictions>):

> Commons considers non-copyright restrictions to be matters for photographers/uploaders or
> reusers and are not grounds for deletion of works from Commons. … It is up to the reusers of
> Commons-hosted media to ensure that they do not violate any non-copyright restrictions that
> apply to the media.

**The UK country page has no non-copyright-restrictions section at all.** Its headings are:
Governing laws · Summary · General · Crown copyright · Ordnance Survey OpenData licence · Open
Government Licence · Non-Commercial Government Licence · Parliamentary copyright · Copyright on
sound recordings · Ordinary copyright · Typographical copyright · Publication right · Database
right · Commissioned works · Copyright tags · Currency · De minimis · Freedom of panorama · Stamps
· Threshold of originality · Signatures · Computer-generated works. No "personality rights", no
byelaws, no mention of Trafalgar Square. So Commons gives London reusers no warning.

**A real one exists and it bites this corpus.** The Royal Parks and Other Open Spaces Regulations
1997 (S.I. 1997/1639), reg. 4, headed "Acts in a Park for which written permission is required",
provides that "Unless the Secretary of State's written permission has first been obtained, no
person using a Park shall— … **(18) take photographs of still or moving subjects for the purpose of
or in connection with a business, trade, profession or employment or any activity carried on by a
body of persons whether corporate or unincorporate**"
(<https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1997/1639/made>). VERIFIED verbatim. That covers Hyde Park,
Kensington Gardens, The Regent's Park, Greenwich Park, Richmond Park, Bushy Park, St James's Park
and The Green Park — i.e. Westminster, Camden, Kensington and Chelsea, Greenwich and Richmond upon
Thames borough pages.

This is **not** a copyright bar and it does not disqualify a Commons file: it is a duty on the
person taking the photograph, not on the person publishing it, and every affected Commons image
was either taken under permission, taken non-commercially, or taken in breach — none of which the
publisher can determine. It is recorded here so the programme does not later discover it and think
it was missed. Treat it as Rome's Italian non-copyright restrictions were treated: noted, not gated.

**What I could not establish:** the Greater London Authority's Trafalgar Square and Parliament
Square Garden byelaws, which I believe restrict commercial photography and filming in those two
spaces without written permission. **london.gov.uk returned HTTP 403 to every request from this
network**, including with a browser user-agent, so I have neither the byelaw text nor its
regulation numbers and I am not asserting its contents. Same 403 blocked the GLA's own Fourth
Plinth programme page, so the *temporariness* of Fourth Plinth commissions rests here on Commons
deletion practice (six independent cases, three different closing admins' actions) and not on the
commissioning body's own words. That is a gap a later wave could close from a different network.

*(The WebSearch budget was not the constraint on this angle — every source above was reachable by
name via `python3` + `urllib`. The constraint was london.gov.uk's 403.)*

## 5. The four worked examples

### The Shard — Renzo Piano, living. **PUBLISH.**

s.62(1)(a) "buildings". Piano's being alive is irrelevant: s.62 is an exception to infringement,
not a rule about term, so unlike the Rome regime there is nothing to compute. The Shard is
additionally a "fixed structure" on the s.4(2) definition, so it qualifies on the widest reading
too. Commons agrees in practice — `File:The Shard from the Sky Garden 2015.jpg` is licensed
`{{Licensed-FOP|{{FoP-UK}}|{{self|cc-by-sa-4.0}}}}` and is a Featured Picture.

**This is the inversion of Rome.** MAXXI was rejected because Zaha Hadid died in 2016; the Shard's
architect is not merely alive but working, and it publishes anyway. Every modern London
building — the Gherkin, the Walkie-Talkie, City Hall, Tate Modern's Blavatnik Building, the
Elizabeth line stations — is in on the same ground. **There is no modern-buildings blocklist for
London.** That is the single biggest practical difference from the Rome programme.

*Non-copyright footnote, not a bar:* the Shard sits in the privately managed London Bridge Quarter
estate, and privately-owned public space in London commonly restricts commercial photography by
contract. Irrelevant to a Commons file shot from a public street or from another building.

### The Fourth Plinth commission, Trafalgar Square. **REJECT.**

It satisfies "sculptures" and it satisfies "in a public place". It fails **"permanently
situated"**, and that failure is dispositive. Fourth Plinth commissions are fixed-term rotating
installations; Commons has deleted them repeatedly and consistently on exactly this ground —
Hahn/Cock, Gift Horse, Alison Lapper Pregnant, Ecce Homo, 'The End', and the 2022–2024 works
(table above).

Three corollaries the photo brief must carry, because each is a trap:

- **The empty plinth is fine.** `File:The Fourth Plinth in Trafalgar Square between exhibits - the
  empty plinth.jpg` and `File:Empty Fourth Plinth.jpg` are on Commons untouched. The plinth is a
  fixed structure of 1841.
- **The other three Trafalgar Square plinths are fine** — Nelson's Column (Railton/Baily, d. 1877/
  1867), Havelock, Napier, the Landseer lions (d. 1873). Permanent, and PD on duration anyway.
- **A Fourth Plinth work that later goes somewhere permanent becomes publishable at its new
  home.** Shonibare's *Nelson's Ship in a Bottle* is publishable outside the National Maritime
  Museum in Greenwich and not publishable on the plinth. This matters directly: the Greenwich
  borough page may legitimately want that image.

**Generalise the rule, do not enumerate the works.** Any fixed-term public-art commission fails —
the Fourth Plinth, the Serpentine Pavilion (INFERRED: built and demolished annually), a
Frieze Sculpture placement in Regent's Park, a Sculpture in the City rotation, a museum loan.
"Temporary" is a property of the installation, not a property of the artist.

### A Banksy on a Shoreditch wall. **REJECT.**

Graphic work, therefore outside s.62 whatever the wall. Living artist. Ownership *prima facie*
Banksy's per *Creative Foundation v Dreamland Leisure* (2015). Illegality is not a defence in
England and Wales. The EUIPO trade mark losses are not copyright rulings. Commons deleted
essentially the whole London category on 31 December 2025. Full reasoning at §3.

**The rule this generalises to is the one that actually matters for London**, because the
blocklist here is not a list of works but a category: **anything painted, printed, stencilled,
projected, tiled-as-picture or applied flat to a surface is out** — Banksy, Stik, Ben Eine's
Middlesex Street alphabet, ROA's Hanbury Street crane, the Brick Lane and Leake Street walls, Bow's
and Croydon's mural programmes, advertising hoardings, painted pub signs, and modern stained glass
(a *Hensher* borderline: hand-made stained glass is a listed example of artistic craftsmanship and
so may be *in*, but it is not a call the finder should make unassisted).

Shoreditch, Hackney, Tower Hamlets, Camden, Croydon and Waltham Forest pages are the ones where
this will bite. Their illustration should be architecture, markets and streetscape, not the wall
art they are famous for.

### Eros, Piccadilly Circus — Alfred Gilbert, died 1934. **PUBLISH, on two independent grounds.**

Properly the **Shaftesbury Memorial Fountain** (1885–1893), surmounted by a winged statue of
Anteros; on the National Heritage List for England its statutory address is "SHAFTESBURY MEMORIAL
FOUNTAIN EROS, PICCADILLY CIRCUS W1", **NHLE list entry 1265625**
(<https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1265625>) — which is exactly the
citation shape the spec's designation gate wants.

1. **Duration.** Alfred Gilbert, 12 August 1854 – 4 November 1934. UK term is life + 70, expiring
   at the end of the 70th calendar year: copyright expired **31 December 2004**. Public domain
   since 1 January 2005 and taggable `{{PD-old-auto}}`.
2. **s.62 independently.** It is a sculpture, and a fountain is also a "fixed structure" hence a
   "building" under s.4(2). Permanently situated in a public place — it has stood in Piccadilly
   Circus since 1893, and its post-war relocation within the Circus does not disturb permanence
   (cf. the *Nelson's Ship in a Bottle* keep rationale).

Belt and braces. This is the easy case, and it is worth stating that **most** London statuary is
this case: the borough corpus is overwhelmingly Victorian and Edwardian.

## 6. Draft rule for the image-sourcing agent

Written to be followed without re-reading the statute, in the four-gate shape Rome used and the
narrowed shape San Francisco used. **This is a draft for the arbitrator/spec author to adopt or
amend — I am not adjudicating.**

> ### London photo rule — UK freedom of panorama (s.62 CDPA 1988)
>
> **Gate 1 — the photograph's own licence.** The Commons file must be CC0, PD, CC BY or CC BY-SA,
> with an author and a source URL. Unchanged from every prior programme. s.62 says nothing about
> the photographer's copyright.
>
> **Gate 2 — what is the subject made of?** Answer in one word before anything else.
>
> | Subject | Verdict |
> |---|---|
> | Building, or any FIXED STRUCTURE, or part of one — tower, terrace, bridge, station, market hall, pier, gate, wall, fountain basin, bandstand | **ALLOW.** The architect may be alive. Do not look up an architect at all. |
> | Free-standing sculpture, statue, monument, war memorial, cast, model for a building | **ALLOW if Gate 3 passes.** |
> | Work of artistic craftsmanship — stained glass, wrought ironwork, ceramics, high-class metalwork, tapestry | **ALLOW if Gate 3 passes.** Borderline; if unsure, treat as REJECT and pick another image. |
> | Anything 2D applied to a surface — mural, street art, graffiti, stencil, poster, painted sign, hoarding, banner, projected image, mosaic-as-picture | **REJECT.** No exception exists. Do not compute a death year; it does not help. |
> | Blue plaque, or any lettered plaque, photographed as the subject | **REJECT.** Uncertain category; not worth the risk. A plaque incidentally visible on a building elevation is fine. |
>
> **Gate 3 — is it PERMANENTLY situated where it was photographed?** Ask of the work, not the
> photographer. REJECT if the work is a fixed-term or rotating commission, a loan, a festival or
> exhibition piece, or anything that has since been removed from that site. Named rejects:
> **any Fourth Plinth commission**, the annual Serpentine Pavilion, Frieze Sculpture placements,
> Sculpture in the City rotations. A work that has MOVED to a permanent home is allowed **at the
> new site only** — Shonibare's *Nelson's Ship in a Bottle* publishes at the National Maritime
> Museum, Greenwich, and does not publish on the plinth.
>
> **Gate 3a — indoors is allowed.** "Premises open to the public" includes ticketed and licensed
> premises: museums, galleries, station concourses, lobbies. Permanence still applies, so a
> permanent-collection sculpture is in and a temporary exhibition is out.
>
> **Gate 4 — the framing test, for anything that failed Gate 2 or Gate 3.** If the rejected work
> is a small incidental element of a wider streetscape, s.31 incidental inclusion may save it. Apply
> the Commons test literally: *if showcasing the work is the point of the image, it is not
> incidental.* When in doubt, choose a different photograph — London is not short of them.
>
> **Gate 5 — the escape hatch.** A work whose artist died more than 70 years ago is public domain
> outright and needs none of the above, including 2D works. Alfred Gilbert (d. 1934), Edwin Lutyens
> (d. 1944), Edwin Landseer (d. 1873) are all clear. This is the route for Victorian statuary and
> for pre-1955 murals.
>
> **Preferred evidence on the file page:** `{{Licensed-FOP|{{FoP-UK}}|<photo licence>}}`, or a
> `{{PD-old-auto}}`-family tag. **A bare photographer's CC licence with no FoP tag is evidence
> about the photograph only and says nothing about the artwork in it** — Commons' UK street-art
> cleanup is demonstrably incomplete (see `File:Image of Banksy street art in Walthamstow,
> London.jpg`, still live under `{{self|cc-zero}}`).
>
> **What the gate should encode as a blocklist.** Unlike Rome (modern buildings) and San Francisco
> (named artworks), London's blocklist is a **category**, not a list: 2D surface works, and
> temporary installations. The only *named* entries worth hard-coding are the traps —
> `fourth plinth`, `banksy`, `serpentine pavilion`, `leake street`, `brick lane` — as a warning
> flag on a filename or description, not as a complete enumeration.

## 7. Answer to the spec's open question, in one paragraph

The spec's working belief was right in substance and understated in one direction. **s.62 CDPA 1988
is materially broader than 17 USC 120(a) and incomparably broader than Italy's nothing**: it
covers buildings *and* — via s.4(2)'s "any fixed structure" — most of the built environment,
plus sculptures, models for buildings and works of artistic craftsmanship, indoors as well as out,
with commercial publication expressly permitted by s.62(3). The architect's or sculptor's date of
death is irrelevant and should not be researched. **The exclusion the spec predicted is real and
exactly as predicted**: two-dimensional graphic works are outside the section, so murals and
street art stay out, Banksy included, and Commons enforces this by deletion as recently as
31 December 2025. **The exclusion the spec did not predict is permanence**: rotating public-art
commissions fail s.62 even though they are three-dimensional sculpture in the most public place in
London, which puts every Fourth Plinth work out of reach. So London is indeed the most permissive
of the three jurisdictions, and the photo brief may say so — but it needs a temporariness gate
that neither Rome nor San Francisco required.
